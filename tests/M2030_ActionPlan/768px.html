Accurate heat targeting and equi...

Labels:

Applying heat with greater precision and control may allow for downsizing of equipment and improvements in product quality.


Tips & advice

Consider using technologies such as microwave heating, lasers and infrared radiant heat are technologies which can enable this.


Infrared heat sourced are ideal for where heat needs to be applied with precision, such as in curing or finishing processes. Electric infrared heat is more efficient than gas infrared and can zone far more effectively. Gas-powered radiant heating is beneficial in that there is a much quicker warm-up period and the costs of gas are lower.

Conduct a facility walk and...

The true cost of waste is often overlooked – such as utilities and energy embodied in the waste, labour time, and other hidden costs – making it a more significant opportunity to tackle than disposal alone. A waste walk and mapping exercise will allow you to see where the waste is being generated, especially hotspots (volume and cost).


Tips & advice

Note: This action relates to physical and resource waste only. For advice about the full ‘lean’ methodology for wastes, which include over-production, excess movement, etc. see the TIM WOODS action.


Often, the main motivations for reducing physical waste arising from your manufacturing processes are the cost of waste disposal and recycling, as well as reputational risks. However, the true cost of waste is often overlooked – such as utilities and energy embodied in the waste, labour time, and other hidden costs – making it a more significant opportunity to tackle than disposal alone.


A waste walk and mapping exercise will allow you to see where the waste is being generated, especially hotspots (volume and cost). This is not the same as a waste audit (see linked action), which will help you identify the sum of your physical waste streams.


Attached to this action as the ‘technical resource’ is a comprehensive guide to waste mapping, from WRAP (Waste and Resources Action Programme) . This really thorough guide also contains templates you can print and use for your walk and mapping, as well as checklists and hints.


Steps


1) Walk around your site

The WRAP guide (see technical resource below) contains a useful blank map, which you could print off to help with this, as well as some examples of how to use the map.

Look for areas where waste is being generated, either wasteful inputs or processes e.g. cut-offs, lost water, lost steam, thermostat set too high.

As you go round, ask yourself questions about processes e.g. ‘is there a place where the product repeatedly gets damaged?’; ‘what happens to the suppliers’ packaging?’

Think about categorising waste types e.g. raw materials, water / effluent, packaging, solid / liquid / gas, hazardous / chemical, energy. The WRAP guide has lots of examples.


2) Start to put in some figures

To begin with this can just be some estimates so you can see where best to focus efforts.

Look first at the inputs – refer to bills to find out how much these cost – e.g. raw materials, electricity consumed.

Then take a look at outputs that are wasted such as units of production and the cost of these.

Consider the levels of waste. Use whatever units make sense – could even be skipfulls at this stage – although for water stick with m³.

If you’re paying for raw materials which don’t get used and you’re paying for their disposal too then the waste is costing you double! This also applies to wasted water.

Talk to colleagues in every part of the site as you go around e.g. 'is that skip emptied weekly or monthly?' And write everything down.


3) Develop your action plan

• The WRAP guide includes useful templates for your action plan. When you prioritise your plan, think

   about: the ease of taking action; the different financial benefits; the amount being wasted.

• Make sure your changes aren’t just moving waste from one area to another.

• The waste map might reveal things about your production processes that you need to redesign.


Tips

• Take lots of pictures. They are a visual prompt for your map and subsequent reduction initiatives, but

   will also help you create a 'before and after' view to demonstrate impact.

• Involve your colleagues. especially those operators who work on the production line. They will have

  great insights as to where and why waste is being generated, but also be key to building support for

  any remedial actions.

• Remember to look down. While you might concentrate on the production process and surrounding

  infrastructure, the floor often reveals a lot.

• Don't forget to review your waste map regularly to account for change in operations and processes, as

  well as to track progress.
